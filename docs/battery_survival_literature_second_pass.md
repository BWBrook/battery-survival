# Battery survival, power management and service-interval planning in camera-trap networks: second-pass source-verified literature review

## Scope and verification notes

This second-pass review treats the supplied Deep Research report as an orientation map, not as verified evidence. I searched around its themes and then prioritised sources that can support a methods- and field-practitioner-first manuscript on battery survival in a Tasmanian camera-trap deployment, using Kaplan–Meier descriptives, Cox proportional-hazards associations, Weibull/AFT prediction, support-limited external validation and service-interval economics.

The strongest direct literature is not a mature camera-trap battery-survival literature; it is a triangulation of: camera-trap observation-process methods, field-practitioner power-management guidance, camera-model/settings performance studies, adjacent ecological sensor-maintenance work, and standard survival/reliability methods. For DOI-bearing sources, identifiers were checked against publisher, PubMed, Crossref-equivalent or other indexed metadata. In several cases, direct `https://doi.org/<doi>` redirection was not available through the browsing environment, so the verification status states the equivalent source used. Sources without a verified stable scholarly identifier are separated in the manual-verification section and are not included in `battery_survival_identifiers.txt`.

# Recent state of the art

- The newest camera-trap methods literature is increasingly explicit about effective effort and imperfect observation, rather than treating camera deployment as a simple count of trap-days. Kühl et al. (2023) and Focardi et al. (2026) are especially useful here.
- The field is also moving toward formal optimisation of camera-trap designs and settings. DeWitt et al. (2023), Barton et al. (2026), Kays et al. (2020, 2021) and Gálvez et al. (2016) support a design-decision rather than rule-of-thumb framing.
- Barros et al. (2024) materially affects the planned manuscript because it places wireless transmission and solar power into a cost-effectiveness frame. The service-interval question should therefore be presented alongside possible technology substitution, not as the only route to reliability.
- Bruce et al. (2025) is important Australian context: camera-trap monitoring is becoming large-scale and long-term, which makes downtime, data saturation, metadata quality and standardisation central scientific issues.
- Cardona et al. (2025) is the closest camera-trap survival-analysis precedent located in this pass. It is not about batteries, but the service-window and interval-censoring structure is directly analogous.
- No 2023–2026 peer-reviewed paper was found that supersedes the planned Tasmanian battery-survival manuscript by modelling camera-trap battery exhaustion itself as the primary survival endpoint. That apparent gap is a defensible novelty claim if worded cautiously.

# Do not overclaim

- Do not write that battery failure always biases occupancy or abundance estimates. Safer wording: battery failure reduces effective effort and can bias inference when nominal effort is mis-specified or when failure is related to ecological or environmental covariates.
- Do not claim that lithium batteries are experimentally proven to be universally superior in camera traps. Safer wording: field guides and practitioner experience commonly recommend lithium, especially for cold or long deployments, but replicated camera-trap chemistry trials remain scarce.
- Do not generalise Tasmanian survival curves to all camera traps. Battery survival is conditional on camera model, firmware/settings, cell chemistry and brand, flash mode, image/video workload, temperature, humidity, vegetation, animal activity and service protocol.
- Do not call observed battery-failure dates exact unless a logger or final image timestamp truly identifies failure. Periodic servicing usually implies right censoring, interval censoring or at best bounded failure windows.
- Do not treat trigger-delay or battery-saving settings as free improvements. They can reduce storage and power demand but may also reduce detections or alter ecological inference.
- Do not imply that flash type has a universal behavioural or detection effect. The literature supports taxon-, context- and objective-dependent effects.
- Do not claim external validation demonstrates universal transportability. Use support-limited phrasing and show where the validation data overlap the development data.
- Avoid 'predict battery life precisely'. Use 'estimate survival probabilities', 'predict risk at a specified service interval', or 'support risk-based scheduling'.

# Suggested manuscript framing

The cleanest framing is that camera-trap ecology has become analytically sophisticated about animal-state and detection-state processes, but remains comparatively heuristic about device-state processes. Battery survival is a device-state endpoint with direct consequences for realised survey effort. Treating battery depletion as censored time-to-event data therefore extends existing camera-trap inferential discipline to a practical bottleneck that field teams already recognise but rarely model formally.

The manuscript should be practitioner-first: report predicted survival probabilities at concrete service intervals, expected lost camera-days under alternative schedules, and cost or labour trade-offs under transparent assumptions. The statistical methods should serve those decisions rather than becoming the centrepiece. The strongest novelty claim is not that nobody has ever discussed camera-trap batteries, but that peer-reviewed camera-trap work rarely treats battery exhaustion itself as the primary reliability endpoint with censoring, validation and service-economics outputs.
