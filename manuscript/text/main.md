# Keeping wildlife cameras running: battery survival and servicing trade-offs in a Tasmanian field network

## Abstract

**Context.** Wildlife cameras record only while their batteries last. When batteries fail between visits, a camera stops observing while its nominal trap-days keep accumulating, so battery choice and servicing frequency determine both the cost and the continuity of camera surveys.

**Aims.** We quantified recorded battery life in a Tasmanian camera network and asked how much observation time a longer-lasting, more expensive battery set must protect to repay its price at a fixed revisit interval.

**Methods.** We applied survival analysis to 3,216 deployment intervals from 489 cameras. Cameras operating at service were treated as censored; for cameras found inactive, the last image gave a minimum confirmed runtime. Camera-effect Weibull and loglogistic models translated this endpoint convention into conditional downtime and break-even costs for observed deployment scenarios.

**Key results.** Median recorded lifetime ranged from 85 days for Procell alkaline batteries to 191 days for Energizer lithium batteries, and white flash was associated with 3.75 times the hazard of infrared flash. Most cameras used more than one brand, and allowing for persistent camera differences changed brand associations little. At 120-day revisits, three cheaper lithium white-flash scenarios matched the Energizer scenario's expected cost when a lost camera-day was valued at 1.37–1.65 illustrative units, about 3% of the Energizer set price. The selected Procell scenario lacked follow-up at 120 days. Thresholds from the two model families were similar at 120 days but diverged more at 90 days. In a held region, Varta batteries lasted longer despite greater flash workload.

**Conclusions.** Under this endpoint convention, lower modelled downtime could offset the higher purchase price of longer-lasting batteries in these scenarios. The balance depends on the revisit interval and deployment conditions, and fitted workload effects did not explain the regional difference.

**Implications.** Expressing a price premium as the value of a lost camera-day makes observation priorities explicit in purchasing and servicing decisions. These conditional scenarios require local prices and validation with installation-time predictors before use as purchasing or servicing guidance.

## Introduction

A camera trap contributes observation effort only while it can record wildlife. Camera trapping is now a standard survey method for terrestrial mammals, and many surveys run as networks of units that are visited only periodically [@Burton2015; @Steenweg2017]. Where effort is counted from deployment and service dates alone, a camera whose batteries fail between visits keeps accruing nominal trap-days while recording nothing, and the survey overstates its opportunity to detect animals. Inference from camera data already depends on the chain of processes linking animal activity, camera operation and recorded detections [@Burton2015]. A flat battery breaks that chain at the camera itself. As networks expand, keeping every unit powered becomes a recurring ecological and logistical cost [@Steenweg2017].

Battery choice and revisit frequency are linked decisions. A cheaper set may need replacing more often, whereas a longer-lasting one can reduce travel or carry a camera through a critical season. The balance is seldom obvious from purchase price or rated capacity. Practitioners have described batteries expected to last for months expiring within days under winter, flash-heavy and image-saturated conditions, with false triggers adding to the drain [@Newey2015]. Theft interrupts coverage in a different way. A survival analysis of camera theft in Tasmania has shown how time-to-event methods can quantify such losses [@Cardona2025]. Battery depletion differs from theft because replacing the batteries restores the next period of observation, which makes the timing of service a decision in its own right. Yet published accounts of battery performance in camera surveys have mostly drawn on field experience, and our search found no previous survival analysis of battery life in a camera-trap network.

Servicing records can quantify this trade-off, but they pose a familiar statistical problem. A camera still running when it is serviced has not reached the end of its battery life; its record shows only that the batteries lasted at least that long. Averaging only the runs that ended in failure, or treating every serviced camera as though it had just failed, would understate typical lifetimes. Survival analysis retains such right-censored records while estimating lifetime distributions and the factors associated with them [@KaplanMeier1958; @Cox1972], and parametric survival models can describe the full lifetime distribution under specified conditions [@Wei1992]. Camera records add a complication at the other end of each run. A camera found dead reveals when it last took a picture, not when its power failed, so its recorded lifetime is a lower bound on how long it actually ran.

Given a lifetime distribution, the number of active camera-days expected under any revisit schedule can be calculated, but a servicing decision needs one further input. The most obvious summary, cost per active camera-day, can only fall or stay level as the interval lengthens: the fixed cost of each visit is spread over more active days, even as the proportion of lost days rises. A defensible schedule therefore requires an explicit judgement about how much a missed day of observation is worth, or how much downtime a survey can tolerate. Reliability engineering has long treated a related problem, balancing the cost of periodic checks against the losses that accrue while failures go undetected [@Barlow1963]. In camera surveys the loss is ecological information, and its value depends on what the survey is for.

Here we use servicing records from a Tasmanian camera network to address three questions. First, how does recorded battery life differ among brands, and how is it associated with flash type, season and image workload? Second, how much must a lost camera-day be worth before a more expensive, longer-lasting battery set becomes the cheaper option at a fixed revisit interval? Third, how far do the answers depend on the choice of lifetime distribution, on repeated deployments of the same cameras and on the region in which the models were fitted? Throughout, we treat the records as evidence of recorded operation, not of the moment each battery failed, and interpret brand differences as associations under this network's deployment practices. The servicing calculations are conditional scenarios. They are intended to show how the trade-off works and what information would resolve it for a particular survey, not to prescribe a schedule for other networks.

## Methods

### Field records and the lifetime endpoint

The dataset contains 4,432 serviced deployment intervals from cameras in six Tasmanian regions and a testbench cohort. Each record describes one interval ending at a service visit, together with the camera's state when it was serviced. The primary analysis uses the 3,216 intervals from 489 camera identifiers in the Central, South East, North West and West regions of the TAS-CAM network. In 1,735 of these intervals the camera was non-functioning at service, and in 1,481 it was still operating. South West (SW; 589 intervals), monitored within the same project and protocol, was held out of model fitting to check how well the fitted relationships transferred to another region. North East (564 intervals, most still operating at service) and testbench records (63, all ending in failure) are described separately (Table S1).

The original outline describes Cuddeback 1279 cameras with eight AA cells and interchangeable flash units; its applicability to the complete analytical dataset needs confirmation.

A record can end in one of two ways. A camera still operating at service is right-censored: its batteries lasted at least as long as the interval. For a camera found non-functioning because of dead batteries or an internal power error, the recorded duration ends at the last dated image. That image shows the camera was working on that day, but it may have continued to run, untriggered, for some time afterwards. The last-image time is therefore a minimum confirmed runtime, not an observed physical shutdown. Service dates for these records, which would bound the gap from above, are unavailable. We assign last-image times as event times and do not impute the gap.

The fitted survival curves describe this operational-lifetime proxy. Downtime calculations make a further assumption, that image production ended at power loss, and so cannot separate unobserved operation after the last image from actual inactivity. The usual assumption of non-informative censoring also requires that visits were not timed in response to battery condition; whether that holds depends on how visits were scheduled.

The predictors were battery brand, flash type (white or infrared), season, region, and two measures of workload: flash images and daylight images per recorded day. Daylight images are total images minus flash images. One missing flash count was replaced by zero under the existing processing rule. Both rates enter the models as $\log(1+\mathrm{images/day})$, which limits the influence of the few cameras with very high image rates. These workloads accumulated during the same interval whose duration is the response, and that duration is also their denominator. Their coefficients therefore describe retrospective associations rather than the effects of exposures fixed in advance. A manager installing a camera does not know how many images it will take. Procell is the only alkaline brand in the training cohort and the other four brands are lithium, so brand and chemistry cannot be estimated as separate effects.

### Survival comparisons

Kaplan–Meier curves and median lifetimes describe recorded survival by brand [@KaplanMeier1958]. At each time since the start of an interval, the curve estimates the proportion of cameras still running, using each censored record for as long as it was observed. The curves reflect the deployment mix as it occurred, without adjustment for other differences between brands, and their ordinary pointwise confidence intervals do not account for repeated use of the same cameras. A region-stratified Cox proportional hazards model then related the lifetime endpoint to brand, flash type, season and both workload variables [@Cox1972]. Stratification gives each region its own baseline hazard, and camera-cluster robust standard errors allow for repeated intervals from the same camera. A hazard ratio compares the instantaneous failure rates of two groups of cameras still running at the same time, with values above one indicating a higher rate. Proportional-hazards diagnostics guided how literally these ratios could be read.

The servicing calculations need a complete, smooth lifetime distribution for each scenario, which the Cox model, with its unspecified baseline hazard, does not directly supply. For these we used accelerated failure-time (AFT) models, in which covariates stretch or compress lifetime by a multiplicative factor [@Wei1992]. A sensitivity analysis compared three distribution families (Weibull, lognormal and loglogistic), with common or brand-specific error scales and with or without region effects. Because the same cameras were deployed repeatedly, matched Bayesian Weibull and loglogistic models were also fitted with and without a normal random intercept for each camera, using the same covariates and priors within each family. The camera term allows some units to run persistently longer or shorter than others under the same batteries and conditions. Management predictions integrate over this variation, so they refer to a new camera drawn from the same population. Supplementary Methods give the model equations, priors, computation, diagnostics and comparison tables.

Training calibration compares predictions averaged over each brand's observed covariates with the Kaplan–Meier estimates. In SW, only the 183 Varta white-flash intervals share a brand/flash combination with the training data. The transfer check is confined to them. It uses models without region terms, because a region-adjusted model has no estimated effect for an unseen region and would need an arbitrary one. As SW belongs to the same project and protocol and the check covers a single battery/flash combination, it is a support-limited test of transfer, not independent external validation.

### Battery selection, servicing and lost observation time

To connect lifetimes with servicing, we consider a repeated cycle. Each cycle begins with an operational camera and a replacement battery set; another set is installed at the scheduled visit after $T$ days, with no intervening rescue visit or restart. For survival function $S(t)$, the expected number of active days in a cycle is $A(T)=\int_0^T S(t)\,dt$, and the expected fraction of the interval lost is $D(T)=1-A(T)/T$. This downtime fraction differs from the probability of failing before the visit, $1-S(T)$. A camera that fails a week before its service and one that fails in its first month both count as failures, but the second loses far more observation time.

Let $K=C_s+C_b$ be the cost of one cycle, combining the service visit and the battery set. Cost per expected active day, $K/A(T)$, can only fall or stay level as a visit is delayed, because $A(T)$ never decreases with $T$. On that criterion alone the longest interval would always appear best, however much observation time it forfeited. It cannot identify an interior optimum. We instead attach an explicit value $c_d$ to each lost camera-day and compare total expected cost per calendar day:

$$
L(T)=\frac{C_s+C_b}{T}+c_d D(T).
$$

The first term spreads the cost of servicing and batteries over the interval, and the second is the expected value of the observation time lost. Unlike the classical inspection problem [@Barlow1963], each scheduled visit here installs a fresh battery set, and the objective is the long-run cost per calendar day over repeated cycles. The value of downtime is a management input and cannot be estimated from battery records. Battery-set costs are illustrative units rather than confirmed Australian-dollar prices: 24 for Procell, 36 for Wallaby, 40 for Ansmann, 48 for Varta and 52 for Energizer. When two options are compared at the same revisit interval and share a per-camera service cost, that cost is identical for both and cancels from the comparison.

The management comparison concerns the five white-flash brands, comparing each cheaper alternative $X$ with Energizer $E$ at revisit intervals of 90 and 120 days. Brands were not spread evenly across regions, seasons and workloads, so each is represented by an observed operating scenario. For each brand/flash group we selected its most frequent observed region–season combination, then the observed workload pair nearest that cell's median transformed workloads (Table S5). This rule avoids predicting for combinations of region and season that never occurred with a given battery. The scenarios still differ in their deployment conditions as well as their batteries. The comparison therefore describes a frequently observed deployment of each brand in this network, not the effect of switching battery at an otherwise unchanged site. Because the workloads were measured retrospectively, the calculations are conditional illustrations, not validated forecasts from information available at installation.

At a fixed interval, the cheaper set saves $C_{b,E}-C_{b,X}$ per cycle but is expected to lose more observation time. With $\bar D$ denoting posterior mean expected downtime, the two options have equal expected cost when a lost camera-day is worth

$$
c_d^*(T)=\frac{C_{b,E}-C_{b,X}}{T[\bar D_X(T)-\bar D_E(T)]}.
$$

The denominator is the number of additional camera-days the cheaper scenario is expected to lose in each cycle. When it is positive, the cheaper scenario has the lower expected cost if a lost day is worth less than $c_d^*$, and Energizer has the lower cost if it is worth more. Uncertainty is described with paired posterior draws from each camera model, which carry shared parameters through both scenarios and integrate over new-camera effects. Supplementary Methods distinguish this expected-loss threshold from its parameter-conditional uncertainty and describe cases without a positive crossing.

Each comparison is accompanied by the numbers of intervals and cameras still at risk in the matching brand, flash, region and season cell. Fewer than 20 intervals or 10 cameras at risk is flagged as thin support, a descriptive warning rather than a validated threshold, and predictions beyond the cell's maximum follow-up are identified separately. The full group size and event count also bear on interpretation. These fixed-interval comparisons ask which battery is cheaper under a given schedule; they do not select an optimal revisit schedule.

## Results

### Battery, flash and workload differences

Recorded battery life varied widely among brands (Table 1; Fig. [1](#fig-km-brand)). Median lifetimes ranged from 85 days for Procell, the only alkaline brand, to 191 days for Energizer, and the four lithium brands were far from uniform: Wallaby and Ansmann fell well short of Varta and Energizer. At 120 days, roughly nine in ten cameras with Energizer or Varta batteries were still recording, compared with about a third of those with Wallaby and fewer than one in ten with Procell. The differences persisted after adjustment for flash type, season and workload within regions. Relative to Ansmann, the hazard of failure was less than a quarter as high for Energizer and less than half as high for Varta. It was more than twice as high for Wallaby, and about seven and a half times as high for Procell. These are brand-associated differences under the conditions in which each brand was used in this network.

Flash type and season were also associated with recorded lifetime. White flash was associated with 3.75 times the hazard of infrared flash (95% interval 2.95–4.77), and winter with a higher hazard than autumn (1.53; 1.33–1.77). Hazard increased with realised flash workload (1.56; 1.30–1.88 per unit of log-one-plus rate) but decreased with daylight-image rate (0.85; 0.74–0.96; Fig. [2](#fig-cox-hazard-ratios)), a counterintuitive pattern discussed below. The global proportional-hazards test gave $p=0.011$, so these ratios summarise associations across follow-up and should not be read as constant multipliers at every age of a battery set.

Most camera identifiers were used with more than one brand: 428 of 489 (87.5%), together contributing 3,027 intervals. The data thus include substantial within-camera overlap between brands. When camera random effects were added to the matched AFT models, brand coefficients retained their directions and changed by less than 0.013 on the log-time scale. Persistent differences between cameras alone therefore do not explain the brand pattern, although differences in deployment timing, allocation or environment could still contribute.

### Battery costs at fixed revisit intervals

At a 120-day revisit interval, the three cheaper lithium white-flash scenarios, Ansmann, Varta and Wallaby, matched the expected cost of the Energizer scenario at lost-day values of 1.37–1.65 illustrative units across the two camera models (Table 2). Per lost camera-day, these thresholds are equivalent to about 3% of the 52-unit Energizer set price. Below a threshold the cheaper set had the lower expected cost; above it, the Energizer scenario's lower modelled downtime outweighed its higher purchase price. The service cost is the same whichever battery is installed and cancels from these comparisons. The thresholds were similar although the savings were not. The Wallaby scenario saved 16 units per set but was expected to lose 11–12 more camera-days per cycle than the Energizer scenario, whereas the Varta scenario saved only 4 units and lost 2.5–2.6 more days. Larger savings came with correspondingly larger losses of observation time, which placed all three thresholds within a narrow band.

At 90 days the thresholds were higher, at 2.31–9.91 units. By then the cheaper lithium scenarios were expected to lose fewer than three camera-days more per cycle than the Energizer scenario, and with so little observation time at stake, a lost day had to be worth more before the premium set repaid its cost. Procell was the exception. Its scenario was expected to lose far more observation time by 90 days and had the lowest threshold at that interval, with 22 intervals from 22 cameras still at risk. The selected Central/winter cell for Procell had no observations beyond 117 days, so its 120-day comparison is withheld.

The two model families agreed closely at 120 days, where their expected-loss thresholds differed by at most 5% in the supported comparisons, but differed by up to 27% at 90 days. There, small downtime differences in the denominator magnify modest differences between the fitted curves. Uncertainty was correspondingly wider: for Ansmann, the Weibull interval was 4.73–16.65 units at 90 days, compared with 1.21–2.41 at 120 days. All of these comparisons condition on the lifetime proxy and retrospective workloads, and none validates a prospective battery choice.

### Sensitivity and regional transfer

Within the supported comparisons, accounting for repeated cameras made little difference, and the choice of lifetime distribution made a moderate one. The expanded AFT comparison, which treats rows as independent, favoured loglogistic over equivalent Weibull models by 11.25 AIC units, with brand-specific scales and region effects improving fit (Fig. [3](#fig-aft-calibration); Table S2). A second diagnostic used conditional servicing intervals, the revisit intervals that minimise expected cost at assumed ratios of cycle cost to lost-day value (Fig. [4](#fig-camera-decisions); Table S3). Adding camera effects changed their posterior medians by at most 1.7 days among the 20 comparisons passing the joint-cell support screen. Differences between families were larger in places, reaching 8.3 days for Energizer infrared at a cost ratio of 50, although that entire brand/flash stratum contained only 25 intervals and eight events.

The held region provided a sterner test, which the fitted models did not pass. Varta white-flash deployments in SW had a median flash workload of 1.66 images per day, against 0.54 in training, yet their median recorded lifetime was longer: 201 days (187–217) against 166 (158–173). At 180 days, observed SW survival was 0.601, compared with 0.289 for the original Weibull model and at most 0.400 among the six no-region models. Greater distributional flexibility did not close the gap between observed and predicted survival in the held region.

## Discussion

### What the battery comparison means for practice

Purchase price is an incomplete guide to what a battery costs to run in the field. Median recorded lifetime varied about twofold across brands in this network, which changes how often a camera must be visited and how much survey time is lost if visits are not brought forward. White flash was associated with much shorter lifetimes than infrared. Battery and flash choices are therefore best evaluated together, and against the observation task, rather than by assigning one endurance figure to every deployment. Choosing infrared solely for endurance may be inappropriate when a study needs colour images or other attributes of white-flash photographs. The higher hazard in winter persisted after adjustment for image workload, and practitioner accounts likewise associate winter deployments with short battery life [@Newey2015]. Most intervals lasted longer than a single season, however, so the season label is only a coarse summary of the conditions a battery set experienced. These records cannot separate temperature from other seasonal differences.

The contrast between Procell and the lithium brands is consistent with the longer field endurance generally expected of lithium cells, but these data cannot estimate a general lithium-versus-alkaline effect. Procell is the only alkaline brand, and the lithium brands differed markedly among themselves. In this network, choosing lithium did not guarantee the endurance of Varta or Energizer. Nor are the scenarios interchangeable treatments, because each combines a battery with a different observed mix of region, season and workload. The frequent use of several brands in the same camera strengthens the comparison, and the small changes after camera adjustment are reassuring about persistent differences between units. Neither turns an observational allocation into a controlled trial.

### Paying for observation continuity

The break-even calculation turns a price premium into a quantity a survey designer can judge: the value of a day of camera operation. Under the fitted lifetime proxy and these scenarios, the break-even value was small. At 120 days, valuing a lost camera-day at more than about 1.7 units, roughly 3% of the price of an Energizer set, was enough to favour the Energizer scenario over all three cheaper lithium scenarios in both models. The selected alkaline scenario had no follow-up at that interval. At 90 days less observation time was at stake, and the premium required a higher value per missed day to recover its cost. Uncertainty in each comparison remains relevant, especially where expected downtime differences are small.

A survey's own running costs offer one way to judge whether a lost day is worth that much. An organisation that pays for travel and staff time to keep a camera operating has, in effect, valued each day of operation at no less than its average cost of delivery. Where a service visit costs several times the price of a premium battery set, that average cost already exceeds the 120-day thresholds reported here. Such reasoning helps to interpret the thresholds, but it does not validate a purchasing rule for new deployments: activity after the last recorded image is unknown, and the workload predictors were measured retrospectively.

An observation requirement should precede the choice of servicing interval. Continuous coverage of a breeding season, a migration or a management intervention may justify a different schedule from a broad inventory in which a missed week matters less. The fixed-interval comparison works within an existing visit schedule; choosing that schedule requires further assumptions about observation priorities, access and the survey's duration. Common service costs cancel here, but costs that differ between alternatives or change with the visit schedule would need explicit treatment. The illustrative prices used here are no substitute for local ones, and the scientific value of a missed day can come only from a survey's own objectives.

### Downtime and ecological effort

Downtime matters ecologically because many analyses of camera data scale detections by survey effort. Under the last-image-as-failure convention, counting every deployed day as active inflates effort by the ratio of nominal to modelled active days, $1/[1-D(T)]$. Because downtime can only accumulate as an interval lengthens, this inflation never decreases as visits become less frequent. Across the supported white-flash scenarios at 120 days, the ratio ranged from 1.01–1.02 for Energizer to about 1.12 for Wallaby (Table S4). For Wallaby, both models predict about 11% downtime: 120 nominal days correspond to approximately 107 modelled active days, with 35 joint-cell intervals still at risk. The Procell scenario is excluded at this horizon because its joint-cell follow-up ends earlier. These calculations use the recorded-lifetime proxy and do not measure the unknown gap after the last image.

The consequences depend on the analysis. For an encounter rate, holding detections fixed while inflating the effort denominator lowers the estimated rate in the same proportion. In occupancy and other detection models, what matters is when the camera was able to detect: an occasion on which it was inactive offered no opportunity for detection and should not automatically be coded as an observed absence. The direction and size of any resulting bias depend on the model, the species and the timing of downtime. They are not in general equal to the effort-inflation factor. Work on camera-trap distance sampling makes a related point about effective survey duration, although there the lost time arises from the delay before a camera can trigger again [@Kuhl2023].

### Endpoint and transfer limitations

Two features of the data qualify the workload associations. The image rates share their denominator with the response, and the last-image endpoint can fall before the camera actually shut down. A camera that takes many daylight images is likely to record its last image close to the moment its power fails. One that is seldom triggered may run for days after its final image, and its recorded lifetime then understates its true runtime by a wider margin. That difference could produce, or contribute to, the apparently protective association with daylight-image rate. The explanation is consistent with the endpoint, but these records cannot distinguish it from deployment differences or other mechanisms, and it does not imply that taking more daytime photographs extends battery life.

The SW result shows why adjusting for workload is not enough to transfer the fitted relationships. Varta white-flash cameras there lasted longer despite roughly three times the median flash workload, whereas the training association would predict shorter lifetimes at higher workloads. Camera age, battery batches and the drier, less humid conditions of the region are possible explanations, but the records cannot establish their contributions. The held-region result limits any universal scheduling recommendation without negating the contrasts observed within the training regions.

Several of these uncertainties could be reduced by routine record-keeping. Retaining service dates, operating status at each visit, camera identity, battery allocation, settings and environmental context would bring battery records into line with wider camera-trap reporting guidance [@Meek2014Reporting]. Service dates would bound the gap after the last image, although they would not reveal the exact moment of shutdown. Where the camera model allows it, a scheduled daily image taken regardless of animal activity would record operation directly and fix the shutdown time to within a day. Prospective servicing guidance will also require predictors available at installation and evaluation on new deployments.

## Conclusions

Field records from a large Tasmanian camera network show substantial differences in recorded battery life among brands and flash types. Linking those lifetimes to servicing costs makes the value of observation continuity explicit: which battery is cheaper depends on the revisit interval and on how much a lost camera-day is worth. Expressed as a break-even value, the premium for a longer-lasting set becomes a quantity that survey designers can weigh against their own observation priorities. These values are conditional on the last-image endpoint and on retrospective operating scenarios. The failure of the fitted models to reproduce survival in a held region cautions against applying them elsewhere without local evidence. Prospective use will require validation with predictors known at installation and a better-resolved record of when cameras stop working.
